## 3 Current Research Status

### 3.1 Business Decision Support Systems

Decision support systems have long been used to assist managers in organizing information, comparing alternatives, and improving the quality of operational and strategic decisions. In early stages, such systems mainly aggregated structured business data, generated reports, and provided numerical summaries. As enterprise analytics developed, decision support expanded from descriptive reporting toward predictive and prescriptive analysis, allowing organizations to make broader use of data in planning and optimization [1][2][3].

Recent studies show that business analytics systems can improve organizational agility, responsiveness, and data-driven capability by helping users interpret large and heterogeneous information sources [1][4]. In practice, however, most business decision support systems are still centered on dashboards, reports, key performance indicators, and predefined analytical views. These functions are useful for monitoring and summarizing business conditions, but they are less effective when users need to decompose complex problems, revise assumptions, compare competing strategies, or explicitly trace the logic behind a recommendation [2][5].

This limitation becomes more serious in tasks that involve uncertain evidence and multiple interacting variables. For example, market entry analysis, business model evaluation, and strategic planning often require the user to consider customer demand, competition, cost structures, risks, and alternatives simultaneously. Traditional dashboard-oriented systems can present these elements separately, but they usually do not externalize the reasoning path through which they are integrated into a final decision. As a result, many existing DSS implementations remain result-oriented rather than process-oriented [3][5][6].

The explainable AI literature has attempted to address part of this problem. Recent work on explainable decision support systems argues that trust, transparency, and interpretability should be treated as central requirements rather than optional enhancements [2][3][7]. Existing studies have proposed taxonomies for explainable decision support, covering visual explanation, rule-based interpretation, sensitivity analysis, and natural-language explanation [2]. These contributions indicate that the field has already recognized the inadequacy of black-box analytical support, especially in contexts where decisions must be justified to managers, regulators, or other stakeholders.

Nevertheless, current explainable DSS research still has an important boundary. Most existing work explains outputs after inference is completed, rather than externalizing the analytical process itself. In other words, many systems can help answer the question “why was this result produced?”, but they are less capable of answering “how is the reasoning process unfolding, and how can the user intervene in it before a final conclusion is fixed?” This distinction is highly relevant to the present topic.

Another noteworthy development is the emergence of canvas-oriented analytical frameworks in business and analytics contexts. Works such as **Analytics Canvas** and **Business-to-Analytics Canvas** demonstrate that canvas-like structures are useful for translating business goals into analytical tasks and supporting communication between business and technical roles [8][9]. These frameworks are not generative reasoning systems, but they establish an important conceptual lineage: the canvas is not simply a visual design preference, but a structured medium for analytical problem formulation and coordination.

Taken together, the literature suggests that business decision support systems have evolved substantially in terms of analytical sophistication and explainability. However, most systems still emphasize presenting results rather than exposing and structuring the reasoning process itself. This gap is one of the main motivations for the study of a generative business canvas system.

### 3.2 Large Language Models and Reasoning

Large language models have rapidly become a major technological foundation for intelligent analytical systems. Existing surveys suggest that they possess strong capacities in natural language understanding, summarization, information organization, planning, and reasoning [10][11]. These abilities make them attractive for business applications, since many business tasks involve unstructured reports, regulatory documents, company announcements, and market narratives rather than purely structured database fields.

One major line of recent work has focused on improving the reasoning performance of large language models. **Chain-of-Thought prompting** demonstrates that models can produce better reasoning when intermediate steps are explicitly elicited. **Self-Consistency** extends this idea by sampling multiple reasoning paths and selecting a more stable answer from the resulting set [12]. **Tree of Thoughts** further moves beyond single-chain inference by treating reasoning as a branching search process [13]. **Reflexion** introduces self-correction through verbal feedback and iterative revision [14].

These methods jointly indicate that reasoning in large language models is no longer treated as a one-shot completion problem. Instead, the literature increasingly models reasoning as a structured process involving decomposition, branching, evaluation, and revision. This shift is especially relevant to business decision support, because business analysis is rarely linear.

At the same time, recent surveys on LLM reasoning also emphasize that stronger reasoning behavior does not automatically imply higher reliability [11]. Trustworthiness and hallucination studies show that large language models may still produce unsupported statements, omit critical intermediate steps, or generate fluent but misleading conclusions [15][16][17]. This weakness becomes particularly serious in business scenarios, where analytical outputs may influence planning, investments, resource allocation, or risk management.

The problem is compounded by the fact that many business tasks involve domain concepts, evolving information, and numerical constraints. A model may appear competent when answering generic questions but still perform poorly when it must interpret a balance sheet, compare financial ratios, reconcile evidence from multiple documents, or reason under domain-specific assumptions. Benchmark studies such as BizBench, FinanceBench, and FinanceReasoning reinforce this point [18][19][20].

Thus, the current state of research supports a balanced conclusion. Large language models have significantly expanded the possibilities of intelligent decision support, especially in tasks involving unstructured business information. However, they remain insufficient as standalone business decision engines. Their reasoning must be strengthened by process control, external knowledge support, and broader evaluation criteria before they can provide dependable support in complex business settings.

### 3.3 Multi-Agent Collaborative Reasoning

Multi-agent collaboration has emerged as one of the most important directions in post-LLM system design. Compared with single-model reasoning, multi-agent systems explicitly distribute tasks across multiple specialized roles, allowing different agents to cooperate, critique each other, and revise intermediate outputs. Recent survey work suggests that multi-agent systems can improve task decomposition, long-horizon planning, perspective diversity, and iterative refinement, all of which are highly relevant to complex analytical tasks [21][22][23].

An important insight from recent literature is that a multi-agent system should not be understood merely as “several agents talking to each other.” Instead, modern multi-agent systems are increasingly structured around several explicit capabilities. The first is **planning**. Planning surveys show that planning has already become a distinct design problem in agent research and is commonly analyzed through task decomposition, plan selection, external module use, reflection, and memory [24].

The second capability is **feedback**. Feedback research categorizes correction mechanisms into internal feedback, external feedback, multi-agent feedback, and human feedback [25]. This is important because collaborative reasoning quality depends not only on the initial analytical path, but also on how effectively the system can detect weak conclusions, revise partial results, and incorporate critique from other agents or users.

The third capability is **tool use**. Tool learning research shows that modern agent systems are being organized around four stages: task planning, tool selection, tool calling, and response generation [26]. For business analysis, this is particularly relevant because many tasks require more than reasoning over parametric model memory. Tool use therefore serves as a bridge between language reasoning and operational analytical capability.

Representative systems illustrate these developments in different ways. **CAMEL** emphasizes role-playing and role-consistent communication [27]. **AutoGen** provides a practical framework for multi-agent conversation and tool-integrated workflows [28]. **MetaGPT** introduces workflow organization and role specialization [29]. **Magentic-One** goes further by proposing an orchestrator that plans, monitors progress, and replans in response to errors while coordinating specialized agents [30]. This line of development indicates that multi-agent systems are evolving from loosely coordinated dialogue structures toward more formal collaborative architectures.

Despite this progress, several limitations remain. Most current multi-agent work is validated in general-purpose task automation, coding, or simulation environments rather than in business decision support settings. In addition, many systems optimize for task completion and benchmark performance, but they do not provide end users with a clear view of the internal collaborative reasoning process. Finally, although feedback and planning are recognized as important capabilities, the evaluation of multi-agent systems still often lacks domain-specific criteria for business usefulness, transparency, and intervention [22][25][26].

For the current topic, these limitations are highly significant. A generative business canvas system should not only benefit from multi-agent collaboration at the backend; it should also expose the structure of that collaboration so that users can inspect, compare, and guide it.

### 3.4 Knowledge Augmentation and Graph-Based Reasoning

Knowledge augmentation has become one of the main responses to the limitations of standalone LLM reasoning. The most influential line of work is **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)**, which retrieves relevant external documents and conditions generation on the retrieved evidence [31]. More recent survey work shows that RAG has evolved from simple retrieval-and-generation pipelines toward more advanced and modular workflows [32]. This evolution is significant because it reflects a shift from static evidence attachment to more structured knowledge integration.

For business decision support, the importance of knowledge augmentation is particularly clear. Business analysis often depends on information that is current, verifiable, and context-dependent, such as annual reports, market news, policy documents, financial disclosures, and industry studies. A purely parametric language model cannot be expected to store all such information reliably or keep it updated. In this context, retrieval is not merely an enhancement; it is a basic requirement for evidence-grounded analytical reasoning.

Recent work further suggests that traditional RAG is still too static for complex tasks. **Agentic RAG** extends the retrieval-generation pipeline by incorporating planning, reflection, tool use, and even multi-agent collaboration into the retrieval process itself [33]. This transforms retrieval from a fixed preprocessing stage into a dynamic component of the reasoning workflow.

In parallel, **knowledge graph enhancement** offers another important line of development. Literature on LLMs and knowledge graphs consistently emphasizes that knowledge graphs provide explicit entities, relations, and paths that can complement the flexible but less structured reasoning style of language models [34][35]. This is useful for business decision support because business reasoning naturally involves relationships among products, markets, stakeholders, channels, costs, opportunities, and risks.

More recent graph-enhanced LLM work also shows that the usefulness of graph knowledge depends on how it is selected. **Knowledge Graph-Enhanced Large Language Models via Path Selection** argues that earlier approaches often relied too heavily on the language model itself to determine which graph knowledge to use, which can be inflexible and may ignore indirectly useful relations [36]. The paper therefore proposes path-level selection as a more structured mechanism for identifying relevant knowledge.

Graph-based reasoning methods deepen this line of work. **StructGPT** shows that reasoning over structured data requires a framework different from ordinary text completion [37]. **Graph Chain-of-Thought** further demonstrates that graph interaction can become an active part of the reasoning loop itself rather than a passive knowledge store [38]. These studies are particularly relevant to a business canvas system because they indicate that graph structures can support both knowledge representation and reasoning organization.

However, the current literature also reveals a boundary. Most knowledge augmentation and graph reasoning studies focus on improving backend faithfulness, retrieval quality, and reasoning performance. They rarely address how selected evidence, graph paths, or intermediate reasoning structures should be presented to users in an understandable and editable way. For the present topic, this means that knowledge augmentation research is necessary but not sufficient.

### 3.5 Visual Analytics and Canvas-Based Interaction

Visual analytics research provides one of the strongest foundations for the interface dimension of the proposed topic. Existing surveys emphasize that visualization in intelligent systems should not be understood only as output presentation. Instead, visual analytics supports understanding, diagnosis, comparison, and intervention [39][40]. This is a crucial distinction for business decision support, because analysts often need to inspect relationships among evidence, alternatives, assumptions, and outcomes rather than simply view a final answer.

Studies on visual analytics for machine learning show that visual representation can serve as an analytical workspace for understanding, diagnosing, and improving intelligent systems [39]. This perspective is particularly relevant to the current topic, because it suggests that an interface can be part of the reasoning environment itself rather than merely the final display layer. Research on visual analytics for explainable deep learning reinforces this view [40].

Recent work on generative AI interfaces extends this argument into the LLM era. Survey studies indicate that text-only chat interfaces are often insufficient for complex analytical workflows [41]. While conversation-based interfaces are convenient for simple requests, they are not well suited for representing branching logic, long-term dependencies, alternative paths, or editable intermediate reasoning units. This is directly relevant to the proposed business canvas system.

Canvas-oriented analytical frameworks strengthen this point from another angle. **Analytics Canvas** and **Business-to-Analytics Canvas** show that canvas-like structures are already used as tools for translating business needs into analytics tasks and for supporting coordination between business stakeholders and technical teams [8][9]. Their importance lies in showing that a canvas is not just a stylistic interface choice. It is already recognized in the analytics literature as a structured medium for decomposition, communication, and specification.

Explainable interface research also supports this direction. Studies in industrial and manufacturing settings explicitly argue that explainability depends not only on the content of explanations, but also on the design of interfaces that match user roles and preserve human oversight [42][43]. Dashboard research adds an important comparison point by showing that interface quality, including format, completeness, and currency of information, can affect decision quality [5]. However, dashboards still mainly improve decisions through better presentation of information rather than by externalizing the reasoning structure itself.

This limitation creates the main opportunity for the proposed research. A generative business canvas can be understood as the next step beyond dashboards and static explanatory interfaces. Instead of merely presenting indicators or textual outputs, it can organize the reasoning process itself as a graph-structured, visible, and editable analytical workspace. In such a system, the canvas is not only a visual container for generated content; its spatial organization, semantic alignment, and reasoning traceability are part of the analytical value of the system.

Taken together, current visual analytics, explainable interface, and canvas literature already provides substantial support for the idea that complex AI-supported analysis should move beyond linear presentation. What remains insufficiently explored is the integration of these interface principles with multi-agent collaboration and knowledge-grounded generative reasoning. This is precisely the space in which a generative business canvas system becomes a meaningful research topic.
